About Me

I'm a software engineer who got into AI the practical way — by building things that had to actually work for real users, not just demo well. Over the past couple of years that's meant writing Java and Spring Boot services, wiring up React front ends, and increasingly, figuring out how to make large language models genuinely useful inside production software rather than bolted on as a gimmick.

What I enjoy most is the middle layer: taking something an LLM can technically do and turning it into a feature that saves someone time, cuts down on manual work, or makes a confusing system easier to navigate. That's shown up in a university AI directory, a financial research copilot, and a healthcare model that helps hospitals plan around patient length of stay.

Outside of pure engineering, I've spent time in data-heavy roles — logistics analytics at DHL, environmental research at ISRO — which gave me an appreciation for how much of "AI work" is really just understanding the data and the people who'll use the output. I'm based in Grand Rapids, Michigan, currently building on GVSU's AI/ML team, and always looking for the next problem worth automating.

Projects

Market Flux – AI-Powered Investment Research & Intelligence Platform

What I set out to do: I wanted to see if I could build a research assistant that didn't just answer questions about the market, but actually helped you act on them — pulling live data, news, and a trading simulator into one place instead of five open tabs.

What I built: An async FastAPI backend that streams responses to a React front end in real time using Server-Sent Events, so answers show up as they're generated instead of after a long wait. Retrieval runs on Gemini paired with PostgreSQL/pgvector, letting the assistant reason over both structured market data and unstructured news context together. I connected it to yfinance, RSS feeds, and Alpaca's paper trading API so the flow goes from "read the news" to "simulate the trade" without switching tools, secured it with Supabase auth, and wrote automated tests covering the API layer, the research logic, and the trading policy engine.

LakerAI Directory – Institutional AI Discovery & Management Platform

What I set out to do: GVSU had AI tools scattered across departments with no central place to find them — I wanted to build something that made discovering and vetting those tools as easy as a search bar.

What I built: A centralized, searchable directory for students, faculty, and staff, gated behind Google auth restricted to university emails. I designed the submission-and-review pipeline on Firestore so anyone can propose a tool while admins review, approve, or edit it through a dedicated admin panel that also handles categories and general content upkeep — all in one workflow instead of scattered spreadsheets. Everything ships through Git-based version control so changes stay tracked and reversible.

Hospital Operational Efficiency & Length of Stay Prediction

What I set out to do: I wanted to find out what actually drives patients staying in the hospital longer than expected — and whether that could be predicted early enough to matter operationally.

What I built: Starting from 100K+ hospital records, I built the full pipeline myself — SQL extraction, feature engineering, exploratory analysis, then modeling — rather than jumping straight to the modeling step. I tested Linear Regression, Random Forest, and XGBoost against each other; XGBoost came out on top with a 0.80 test R², 0.82 MAE, and 1.05 RMSE. The real payoff wasn't the model score itself, but pulling out the specific factors tied to longer stays so hospital teams could actually plan resources around them.

U.S. Presidential Election Analysis (1976–2020)

What I set out to do: A side project born out of curiosity — how has party support in the U.S. actually shifted over four decades, region by region?

What I built: Using R and the tidyverse, I pulled apart decades of voting data and built a set of ggplot2 visualizations that made those long-run patterns easy to actually see rather than just read as numbers in a table.
